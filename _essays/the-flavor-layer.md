The earlier chapters describe the structure. Proteins in separate bags, rice in the cooker, veg prepped and containerized. Each component is independent, firewalled, swappable. The system handles four constraint profiles at one table. It works.

But components on a plate are not a meal. A piece of chicken, a scoop of rice, and some vegetables is technically dinner. It's also the kind of dinner that makes the cook stop caring about cooking. The structure keeps everyone fed. The flavor layer is what makes it worth feeding them.

---

Pickled onions live in my fridge at all times. Quick pickles. Vinegar, salt, sugar, whatever spice fits the cuisine that week. They take ten minutes to make and they last for days. Same with pickled chiles and cucumbers.

These are standing infrastructure. They sit in the fridge the way the batch rice sits in the fridge. They're ready when I need them and they cross every cuisine I cook. Pickled red onions go on Mexican, on Mediterranean, on a sandwich. Pickled chiles go on Thai, on American, on anything that needs heat and acid. They're a flavor component that doesn't belong to one cuisine. They belong to the kitchen.

The bright note matters. A plate of batch-cooked components can taste flat. The protein is fine. The rice is fine. The veg is fine. But fine is how the cook falls through. Fine is the ten safe meals on rotation that make you stop wanting to be in the kitchen. A quick pickle on top of the plate is the acid, the crunch, the thing that turns fine into something I actually want to eat. That's a small thing, and it's the kind of small thing that keeps the system running because it keeps the cook interested.

---

Sauces are the same decomposition I use on everything else.

A sauce has a structure: oil, aromatics, spices, a flavorful liquid, a thickener. That's it. Every cuisine runs through the same formula with different inputs. The technique is identical every time. What changes is which aromatics, which spices, which liquid.

Mexican: oil, garlic, cumin, chili. Bloom the spices in the oil until they're fragrant. Add stock or lime juice. Thicken with a cornstarch slurry.

Thai: oil, garlic, ginger, lemongrass. Same bloom. Coconut milk as the liquid. Same thickener or reduce it down.

Sicilian: olive oil, garlic, basil. Crushed tomato. Reduce. No thickener needed because the tomato does the work.

The formula transfers across every cuisine I cook because I decomposed how sauces work. The same way I decomposed gluten to understand what it does structurally, I broke sauces down into their components so I could rebuild them in any direction. Oil carries fat-soluble flavor. Aromatics build the base. Spices define the cuisine. The liquid is the body. The thickener is the texture. Once you understand the function of each piece, you can assemble a sauce in any cuisine without a recipe.

The food science helps. Xanthan gum, cornstarch slurries, reduction, emulsification. These are the technology layer applied to flavor. I learned them the same way I learned GF baking: by studying what the material actually does and figuring out which tools replicate the function I need.

---

The spice profiles are where the biography comes back.

The aromatics I reach for are the ones I grew up smelling. Garlic and basil from my grandmother's sauce on East 1st Street. Cumin and chili from the Mexican food that was everywhere in Brooklyn. Ginger and lemongrass from Thai restaurants with my wife. Each cuisine's spice profile traces to a place and a person, the same way the cuisine DNA chapter describes.

When I'm building a sauce for Thai week, I'm pulling from that biography. The lemongrass isn't a recipe instruction. It's a flavor I know because I've eaten it in specific places with specific people. The decomposition gives me the formula. The biography gives me the inputs. Both are necessary. The formula without the biography is a textbook. The biography without the formula is guessing.

---

At the table, the flavor layer is accommodation.

A sauce on the side is opt-in. My daughter can take it or leave it. My son might try the chicken with the sauce one night and without it the next. The pickle is an option, not a requirement. The spice profile is in the component, but the intensity is controllable because nothing is pre-mixed into a single pot.

This is the modular system applied to flavor. The structural chapters describe how to keep components separate so one rejection doesn't take down the meal. The flavor layer adds a second dimension: even within accepted foods, the sensory experience can vary night to night. A pickle changes the texture. A sauce changes the flavor profile. Dehydrated fruit is the same fruit with a completely different mouthfeel. These are alternate sensory paths to the same ingredient.

I dehydrate fruit when it's approaching expiration. That's opportunistic, not planned. But the result is a snack with a different texture than the original, and sometimes a different texture is the difference between a food that works and a food that doesn't. ARFID narrows the band of what's acceptable. Preservation widens it without changing the ingredient.

---

The flavor layer is where the cook gets to be a cook.

The structural system (containers, firewalls, forgiving tools) is engineering. It solves the logistics of feeding four people with incompatible needs. The flavor layer is where the cuisine rotation becomes real. It's the difference between "I made Thai components this week" and "I made a Thai sauce from garlic and ginger and coconut milk and it smelled like that place on Atlantic Avenue."

The formula is the same every time. Oil, aromatics, spices, liquid, thickener. The inputs change with the cuisine. The biography determines the inputs. And the pickles sit in the fridge through all of it, ready to go on anything, the one constant across every rotation.
