The critique room at SVA had work pinned floor to ceiling. Not finished work. Process. Ink studies, type specimens, torn compositions reassembled with tape. You could read the walls like a timeline of someone figuring something out. That room is where I learned to think about design, and it happened in two classrooms that taught two completely different things.

Christoph Niemann and Nicholas Blechman taught Visual Communication together. The class operated on one principle: the primary job of a designer is visual communication. A mark carries feeling and concept, carries meaning beyond form. Swiss grid, typographic hierarchy, information density. These were structural tools for making something legible, for organizing content so the viewer could actually receive it.

What Niemann and Blechman drilled into us was precision. Every stroke had to do work. If a mark didn't communicate, it didn't belong. You couldn't hide behind aesthetics or decorate your way out of a weak idea. The critique was always the same question asked different ways: what is this saying? If you couldn't answer in one sentence, the piece wasn't done.

I internalized that. Communication is the job. Structure is the mechanism. The mark is never decoration.

Then there was James Victore.

Victore's class was a different room entirely. If Niemann and Blechman were about rigor, Victore was about trust. Be fiercely yourself. Apply the aesthetics that speak to the project, but apply them as you. The class was really about stance. His own work was loud, hand-lettered, confrontational, but the lesson was always about honesty. Whatever your instinct is, trust it. Then push it further.

I spent time at Victore's studio in Beacon, working with him directly. That's where the lesson landed differently than it did in the classroom. In the studio you could see how his fierceness wasn't performance. It was just how he worked. Every decision came from a clear sense of who he was and what the project needed. No committee. No second-guessing.

These two principles together did something neither one could do alone.

Niemann and Blechman gave me the discipline: communicate. Every mark carries meaning. Structure serves the viewer. And Victore gave me something harder to articulate. That the communication has to come through you specifically, through your particular instinct and eye, or it's just competent.

Put them together and you get something I've been operating on for years without naming it. The mark carries meaning (that's the Niemann and Blechman side), and the meaning comes through your particular hand, your particular eye (that's the Victore side). I think the combination is what people mean when they talk about having a voice. At the time I was just trying to figure out what I was actually saying.

I have to be honest. I'm not synthesizing all of this completely until now. It has been instinctual. Two classrooms, two principles, and I've been running on the combination for over fifteen years without stopping to say what it was. I only started putting language to it recently. The operating instinct goes back to those rooms.

I should say: I never walk into either of those rooms without Judith Wilde. She found me at Kingsborough Community College before any of this, saw a sketchbook, took over my schedule, and spent two years steering my development before sending me to SVA. So the real sequence is three people across three rooms, and what came out of all of them is how I think about communication, about structure, about when structure serves and when it constrains. I'm only recently figuring out how to talk about it.
