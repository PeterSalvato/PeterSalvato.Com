I get up and go straight out of the bedroom. Take my medications. Get water boiling. That's the first thing. Before the kids, before the relay, before anything that requires me to be a parent. I get my coffee started because the rest of the morning depends on a person who can function and that person needs caffeine to exist.

Then I go into each kid's room and make first contact. Get them started getting up. My wife makes her way out and makes second contact. By then I've washed the lunch containers from last night that didn't get dealt with because by bedtime there was nothing left. I start on my daughter's lunch. Depending on who's present first, one of us gets both breakfasts on the table. My wife starts my son's smoothie because that's what we figured out. A smoothie is the only thing we can reliably get into him during the school day. Sixth grade, now seventh grade, and deep autistic burnout have made school so strained that solid food at lunch is not happening. The smoothie goes in his backpack. My daughter gets on the bus. I drive my son. I walk in the door at 9:30, half an hour late for work, and start my day from there.

That's the system. That's as tight as it gets.

---

The reliable frictions are the things that go wrong the same way every morning.

My son can't get out of bed. Autistic burnout has wrecked his sleep. He's up until 2 AM and then I'm trying to wake someone who got four hours and can't regulate anything. Some mornings he makes it to the table. Some mornings the bed wins and we're in triage.

My daughter can't eat breakfast. Cereal is about as far as she'll go in the morning and some days even that doesn't work. ARFID doesn't care that it's 7:15 and the bus comes at 7:45. The food is wrong today in a way she can't explain and I can't fix.

One or both kids are dysregulated enough that a fight starts between them. Two neurodivergent kids who didn't sleep well and can't eat well, in the same kitchen, at the same hour. The friction is predictable. Which morning it happens is not.

My wife or I are so depleted that we get short with someone. The parent accommodation fails. The person running the system falls through it, same as the cook in the kitchen, same pattern, different hour.

---

The kitchen has firewalls built into the material. Containers, shelves, sealed bags. The boundaries are physical and they don't depend on anyone's executive function to hold. The morning doesn't have that. The variables are people and people are different every single day.

I can't containerize my son's sleep. I can't firewall a fight between two dysregulated kids. I can't batch my wife's capacity or my own.

But what I can do is run parallel tracks.

My son's morning and my daughter's morning are separated enough that one derailing doesn't take the other down. If my son can't get out of bed, my daughter still gets breakfast and makes the bus. If my daughter rejects cereal and I'm improvising, my wife is handling my son's smoothie and backpack. The handoffs between us mean if one parent gets pulled into a crisis with one kid, the other parent covers the other kid.

The firewalls are human instead of mechanical. They're thinner. They fail more often. But they're there. And the proof is that most mornings, both kids get out the door.

---

The accommodations are small and stacked. None of them is a solution. Each one absorbs one failure.

The iPad at the breakfast table. Same as dinner. The screen gets calories in. My son with a screen and cereal in front of him is a kid who might eat 300 calories before school. My son without the screen is a kid staring at food he can't face.

The smoothie instead of a packed lunch. We tried sandwiches. We tried containers of leftovers. We tried everything the school lunch playbook suggests. The smoothie is what survived. It's portable, it's calorie-dense, he can drink it between classes, and it doesn't require him to sit in a cafeteria and eat while overstimulated.

The boiling water before anything else. My own accommodation. The cook who falls through the system does it at dinner. The parent who falls through the system does it at 7 AM. The coffee is the one thing I do for myself before I start doing everything for everyone else.

First contact, second contact. The relay between me and my wife. A negotiation that happens in real time based on who's present, who has capacity, who's already dealing with a crisis. Some mornings I do 80% and she does 20%. Some mornings it's the reverse. The system flexes because it has to.

---

I want to be honest about this. The morning is barely controlled chaos. The accommodations I've described are real and they help and most mornings they're enough. But enough means both kids got out the door and I'm at my desk by 9:30 already depleted. That's the win condition. A survivable morning.

The kitchen chapters describe systems I built that work. Forgiving tools, containerized storage, cuisine rotation. The engineering holds. The morning is where the engineering is thinnest because the material is the most unpredictable. A kid in autistic burnout is not a sous vide bag that holds at temperature until you deal with it. A kid in autistic burnout is a person who needs something different every morning and you have to read which morning this is while you're still waiting for the coffee to kick in.

That's attunement under the worst conditions. No capacity, no time, no margin. You read the room anyway because the room is your children and they need you to see where they are before you ask them to do anything.

Some mornings I read it right and the accommodations hold and everyone gets where they need to be. Some mornings I miss it and the cascade happens and I'm driving to school with a kid who didn't eat and a knot in my chest. The system doesn't prevent that. It just makes it less frequent. And when it happens, the other kid's morning is still intact because the tracks were parallel.

That's what the firewalls buy you when the firewalls are human. Not perfection. Containment.
