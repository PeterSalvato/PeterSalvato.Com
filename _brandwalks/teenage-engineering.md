opener: |
  Same eight lenses. Teenage Engineering is a brand I've watched for years because the product design is so obviously considered. Every object they make looks like it was argued over at the millimeter level. So I wanted to see whether that obsession translated to the web presence, or whether the site was just a storefront for beautiful hardware.

  What I found is that the product pages are extraordinary and almost everything around them is either missing or thin. That gap is the whole reading.
analysis: |
  Teenage Engineering builds every product page like it's a standalone gallery. The OP-1 field gets patient editorial treatment, a narrative about evolution, detailed illustration of internal architecture, language like "we didn't want to alter the original OP-1 too much, because we think we got it more or less right the first time." The EP-133 opens with a Muhammad Ali quote and positions a $329 sampler as creative ambition. The Choir page turns eight wooden dolls into characters with names and backstories. Each product page is a self-contained world with its own atmosphere. And each one holds.

  The problem is between those worlds. There's no /about page. The 404 is a raw HTTP error with no branding. The /stories URL returns nothing. The "Now" section exists as editorial content (developer interviews, artist profiles, musician features) but you'd only find it if you knew to look for it. The company describes itself exclusively in Japanese on the homepage, which is either a deliberate choice for a Stockholm-based studio or an oversight that nobody caught. The press page has 53 releases dating back a decade and no company narrative to frame them. Teenage Engineering is a studio that has spent ten years building extraordinary [fidelity](/practice/voice-governance/) into individual surfaces and almost no [structural scaffold](/practice/input-first-design/) connecting them.

  I think this is a specific kind of [coherence](/practice/lens-extraction/) failure. Each product page is attuned to its own object. The OP-1 page understands the OP-1. The OB-4 page understands the OB-4. But nobody is attuned to the visitor who arrives at the site without a product already in mind. The Field System page tries to solve this ("made to work together as a unit, but powerful enough to perform wonders as standalone products") and that's the one piece of language on the entire site that reads like someone thinking about the ecosystem instead of individual objects. One page out of dozens.

  The AI question here is practical. Teenage Engineering generates product content at a high level of craft. The editorial voice on the "Now" section is warm and specific. The product copy balances technical depth with aspirational framing. But as they scale (more products, more collaborations, more content), the missing scaffold becomes load-bearing. Without a structural system connecting the pieces, every new product page is another standalone room with no hallway. AI tools can generate more rooms. They can't generate the [attunement](/practice/practitioner-in-the-loop/) that decides how the rooms connect. Someone needs to be reading the whole building, and right now nobody is.
