opener: |
  Same eight lenses. But this time, two websites.

  Carhartt (carhartt.com) is a 137-year-old workwear company out of Detroit. Family-owned, four unionized American factories, triple-stitched 12-ounce cotton canvas. Hamilton Carhartt started it in 1889 with two sewing machines and five workers. The slogan was "Honest value for an honest dollar." Current revenue is $1.8 billion.

  Carhartt WIP (carhartt-wip.com) is a European streetwear label. Swiss designers Edwin and Salomee Faeh got a license in 1994 to create their own clothing under the Carhartt name. Stores in Paris, Milan, London, Barcelona, New York, Los Angeles. Collaborations with Comme des Garcons, A.P.C., Vetements, Junya Watanabe, A Bathing Ape. Marketed alongside Stussy and Supreme.

  Same logo. Same name. Two completely different companies selling to two completely different people through two completely different websites built on two completely different tech stacks. I wanted to see what happens to [brand coherence](/practice/lens-extraction/) when the brand literally forks itself.
analysis: |
  The first thing I noticed is that these are not two halves of one brand. They are two separate organizations that happen to share a trademark. Carhartt mainline runs on Angular. WIP runs on React. Carhartt's design system uses Tungsten and Trade Gothic typefaces with a blue primary color (#055f9f). WIP uses its own type system, its own color palette, its own campaign language. "Softening the Edges" and "Snake Camo" are WIP campaigns. "True to This" is mainline. There is no shared visual scaffold. No shared navigation. No acknowledgment that the other site exists.

  This is not a Red Wing situation, where Work and Heritage live under one roof and the problem is internal navigation. Carhartt and WIP are structurally separate companies that share a logo the way cousins share a last name. The question is whether that matters.

  I think it matters a great deal, and the reason is the audience in the middle. There is a person who wears a Carhartt Detroit Jacket to a construction site and a different person who wears a Carhartt WIP Detroit Jacket (yes, same name, different product, different price) to a gallery opening. Those two people know the other exists. They both typed "Carhartt" into a search engine. One of them landed on a $209 European streetwear piece. The other landed on American workwear with ASTM safety ratings. Neither site explains the relationship. Neither site links to the other. Neither site says "there are two of us."

  The [fidelity](/practice/voice-governance/) of each individual site is adequate on its own terms. Mainline Carhartt is a competent workwear e-commerce store with strong heritage material (the Tommy Boy Records hip-hop crossover in 1990, the Alaska loyalty phenomenon, the COVID mask production pivot). WIP is a competent streetwear store with strong cultural positioning (SoundCloud in the footer, Audio Archives editorial content, subculture immersion as a stated strategy). Each site knows its audience. Neither site knows its sibling.

  The heritage story is where the drift gets structural. Hamilton Carhartt started making railroad workers' overalls in Detroit. That origin belongs to the mainline company. But WIP uses the Carhartt name, the Carhartt logo, and a product line that references workwear silhouettes (the Detroit Jacket, the Double Knee Pant, the Michigan Coat). WIP's entire aesthetic vocabulary is borrowed from the mainline's functional history. The Michigan Coat on WIP costs EUR 189 and comes in "Hamilton Brown." That color name is a direct reference to the founder. WIP is building on a foundation it doesn't explain or credit on its own site.

  I think about what this means for [accommodation](/practice/accommodation-design/). A brand system is a set of structural commitments that hold meaning stable across contexts. Carhartt's brand system has no structural commitments across its two contexts. A visitor who enters through WIP arrives at a streetwear world with skateboarding, graffiti, and Comme des Garcons collaborations. A visitor who enters through mainline arrives at a workwear world with flame-resistant clothing and ASTM certifications. The founder's name appears in both as a color swatch. That is the entire connective tissue.

  The mainline site has a specific problem with its Angular SPA architecture. The homepage title reads "Durable Workwear, Outdoor Apparel & Gear" but most content loads dynamically and resists any attempt to read it as a static document. The CSS reveals a "Santorini" theme with defined breakpoints and a component library, but the actual content (hero images, promotional banners, product messaging) lives behind JavaScript rendering. For a brand that prides itself on solidity, durability, and honest construction, the website is architecturally opaque. You cannot see what the site says without executing its code. The boots are triple-stitched. The website is single-threaded.

  WIP's site is more legible. The homepage shows campaigns ("Softening the Edges"), product grids with prices in euros, and an Audio Archives section that connects the brand to music culture. The footer includes SoundCloud alongside Instagram and YouTube. The newsletter says "Sign up for the latest news from Carhartt WIP." The copyright reads "Work in Progress." WIP knows what it is. It just does not know where it came from, or it has decided not to say.

  As AI tools make it easier to generate product content and campaign copy, the distance between these two sites will widen. Mainline's product catalog (workwear with safety specs) and WIP's product catalog (streetwear with campaign narratives) will each scale independently. The already-thin connection between them will become thinner. Someone who types "Carhartt" will land in one world or the other based on their search history and ad targeting, and the brand will have two completely separate identities with no [attunement](/practice/practitioner-in-the-loop/) between them.

  The pieces hold. Each site works on its own. The joints between them do not exist, because nobody built joints. And nobody is reading the system as a system, because the system was never designed to be one. It grew into two separate organisms that share a name, and the name is doing all the structural work that architecture should be doing.
