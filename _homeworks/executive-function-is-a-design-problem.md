A kid in my classroom gets a compound instruction. Take out your notebook, turn to page twelve, copy the problem from the board, and start working on it. Four steps. By the third step the first two are gone. He's holding the notebook but he doesn't know what page. He's looking at the board but he doesn't know what he's looking for. The output is a kid sitting at a desk with an open notebook and a blank stare, and from the outside it looks like he's not trying.

He's trying. There are too many steps and not enough processing power. The later parts get dropped. The output isn't what it should be.

I taught self-contained special education in Sunset Park, Brooklyn. Twelve IEPs at once. Every student with a different profile. The instruction that worked for one kid overwhelmed another. The pacing that kept one engaged left another behind. Every day was the same problem: how do you design a task so the person receiving it can actually complete it?

---

The answer is decomposition. Break the compound instruction into single steps. Take out your notebook. Good. Turn to page twelve. Good. Now look at the board. See the problem in the red box. Copy that problem onto your page.

Same information. Same task. The difference is that each step is small enough to hold. The kid completes one, gets confirmation, and moves to the next. The cognitive load at any moment is one thing, not four.

This is task chunking. Each step is an atomic unit. Mastered independently, sequenced deliberately. The same approach as modular software. The same approach as the containerized kitchen. You break the process into pieces small enough that a failure in one doesn't cascade into the others. The kid who loses step three still completed steps one and two. The recovery is local.

---

I didn't sit down one day and decide to apply the classroom methodology to my kitchen. That's how it sounds when I describe it, but that's the cleaned-up version. The real version is that I was doing the same thing everywhere and I gradually noticed it was the same thing.

In the classroom, I decomposed tasks for kids who couldn't hold compound instructions. At my desk, I decomposed software problems into smaller functions because I couldn't hold the whole system in my head. In the kitchen, I broke dinner into components because trying to cook one monolithic meal while managing four constraint profiles was failing every night.

The operation was always the same: too many steps, not enough processing power, break it down. I was running it on every material I touched. The classroom, the code, the kitchen. The recognition that it was one pattern across all of them happened slowly, over years of doing it without a name for it.

---

The harder realization was about myself.

I grew up in an environment that wasn't built for the way I process things. Most of us did. The accommodation wasn't there, so you learn to push through. You build calluses. You develop workarounds that cost you energy but produce results that look close enough to normal. And you internalize that as how it's supposed to be. The effort is invisible. The output is passable. Nobody questions it, least of all you.

I watched my kids struggle with things that small structural changes would fix. My son can't hold a morning routine in his head, so we externalized it. My daughter can't eat food that crosses certain sensory thresholds, so we modularized the plate. For them, the accommodation was obvious. They needed it and we built it.

It took longer to see that I needed the same thing. The kitchen failing at 5 PM wasn't a discipline problem. It was a processing problem. The same one I'd been accommodating in my students for years. Too many steps, not enough working memory, the later parts get dropped.

The learned ableism is the part where you know how to build the accommodation for everyone else but you don't think you deserve it yourself. You push through because that's what you were taught to do. You internalize the struggle as character. And at some point, maybe while you're building your son's morning routine or redesigning the batch cook for the third time, you realize it can be lighter. It doesn't have to destroy you to put together a good life.

---

It's also how I learn anything new.

When I decided to learn hydroponics, I didn't take a course. I decomposed the domain. What's involved? Plants, water, no dirt. The water has to move, so pumps. Aquarium pumps? OK, what kind. Pipes to carry the water. Nutrients in the water. Light requirements. I used AI to fill the gaps: help me understand how these pieces fit together for a hydroponic system. What don't I know. Then I started identifying each piece, figuring out what it does in the system, and building from there.

Same process for GF baking. Same for sous vide technique. Same for food science. Same for every domain I've entered. The tool changes. It was microfiche, then books, then web searches, then AI. The operation doesn't change: decompose the domain, identify the parts, figure out how they connect, start building immediately. The learning and the building happen at the same time. You don't study then apply. You study by applying. Hands and brain together, talking it out loud, making as many connections as you can.

---

The systems in this series exist because pushing through is a design failure. The batch cook, the containerized storage, the forgiving tools, the AI co-pilot, the firewalled morning routine. Each one is an accommodation. Each one reduces the cognitive load at the point of execution so the person doing the work can actually complete it.

The kid in my classroom needed the instruction broken into steps. The cook in my kitchen needed the same thing. The difference is that in the classroom I could see it immediately. In my own life it took years of building systems before I understood what I was building and why.

I decompose because that's how I survive the complexity. I didn't learn it as a methodology. I lived it as a necessity, in a classroom in Sunset Park, and then in every room I've been in since. The material changes. The operation is the same. And the operation is accommodation: figure out what the system actually needs, and build structure that meets it instead of asking the person to be different.
