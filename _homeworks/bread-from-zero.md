I had never baked anything before celiac. I didn't come to gluten-free baking as a baker who lost an ingredient. I came to it as someone who had never baked at all, starting with the hardest possible version of the material.

I needed bread. Sandwiches were a load-bearing meal in this house. Lunch for the kids, quick dinners, something reliable I could always fall back on. Store-bought GF bread existed but it was expensive and most of it tasted like compressed rice dust. If I was going to have real bread in this kitchen, I had to learn how to make it.

---

I didn't follow a curriculum. I immersed from every direction at once. TikTok videos, YouTube, Instagram, blog posts. I was looking for overlaps. What do all these recipes have in common. What flours keep showing up. What brands. What supplements and additives. I read every gluten-free baking book I could find. Most of them were recipe-based, which makes sense if you want to make a specific thing. But starting with the finished product doesn't work for me. That's backwards. I needed to understand the ingredients.

So I decomposed the flour. I started looking at the GF flour blends on the market and figuring out what they were made of. The ones that were mostly rice flour came out sweeter. Other starches changed the profile. There were imported blends from Italy made from wheat with the gluten extracted, and eventually an American company came out with one that used the same approach. That was the one I landed on.

Then it was technique. Which bread techniques existed because of gluten and which ones worked regardless. How hydration behaves differently without gluten development. What proofing does when there's no gluten network to trap the gas. I read about xanthan gum and psyllium husk as structural replacements. I studied what each one contributed and when you'd use one over the other.

The whole time I was reading, I was baking. Making messes. Bad loaves, dense loaves, loaves that looked right and tasted like nothing. The learning and the building happened at the same time. I didn't study first and then apply. I studied by applying. Hands and brain together, adjusting after every failure.

It started to click. Then it got good.

---

I was making batards mostly. The shape worked for sandwiches and for dipping with soups and stews. Reliability was important because bread was infrastructure in this house, the same way batch rice and pickled onions are infrastructure. If the bread was there, a dozen meals became possible. If it wasn't, the options narrowed.

Then I switched to rectangular loaf tins. The shape was more practical for sandwiches. The slices were uniform. The loaf fit in a bag for the kids' lunches. It became the default: bake on the weekend, slice for the week, sandwiches every day.

---

Then my son stopped being able to eat sandwiches at school.

The school wasn't accommodating his needs. Sixth grade, then seventh. The stress built up until eating solid food at lunch became something he couldn't do. The cafeteria was overstimulating, the social pressure was constant, and the anxiety was so high that chewing and swallowing a sandwich was more than his body could manage.

We switched to smoothies. A smoothie is easier to consume under stress. He can drink it between classes. He doesn't have to sit in the cafeteria. The calories get in without requiring him to eat in an environment that's actively working against him.

The sandwiches started coming home untouched. I was throwing bread away every day. Eventually I stopped baking because the bandwidth wasn't worth it when the need had moved. The bread was good. The problem was that a system outside my control had changed what my son could tolerate, and no amount of kitchen engineering could fix what was happening at school.

---

That's the limit of the systems in this book. They cover the house. The kitchen, the morning routine, the batch cook, the firewalls. Inside these walls I can attune to every surface and build accommodation for every profile. But the moment my kid walks out the door, the accommodation depends on someone else. And when someone else doesn't provide it, the fallout lands back in my kitchen.

The smoothie is the accommodation I could build for a problem I couldn't fix. The school is stressful. I can't change that. But I can change what goes in his backpack so that eating during the school day is still possible even when everything about the environment says it shouldn't be.

---

The same thing plays out for me outside the house. Celiac doesn't stop at my front door. If we're going to a barbecue, there will be buns I can't eat. If we're at the beach and everyone wants ice cream, there might be nothing safe. So I pre-accommodate. I build things into the meal prep that I can take with me. I eat before I leave. I bring a backup.

When I work the system well enough, I don't feel deprived. The accommodation is invisible. I'm at the barbecue eating food I brought and nobody notices and I don't feel like I'm missing anything. That's the same principle as the kitchen systems: when they work, they disappear. The family doesn't experience the methodology. They experience a home. And I don't experience the celiac. I experience a barbecue.

When I don't work the system, when I didn't prep, when I forgot to bring something, I'm standing at a cookout watching everyone eat while my stomach is empty and the only options have gluten in them. The system doesn't work retroactively. It has to be in place before I need it. Same as the batch cook. Same as the morning routine. The structure has to exist before the moment arrives.

---

I'll bake again when the need comes back. The knowledge is still there. The flour blends, the hydration ratios, the technique. It doesn't expire. And that's the thing about learning a domain through decomposition instead of through recipes. Recipes are instructions for a specific outcome. Understanding the material means you can rebuild in any direction when the need changes. The bread stopped mattering. But I still know how flour works, and when the need comes back around I won't be starting from zero again.
